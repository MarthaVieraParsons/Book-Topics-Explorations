# Chapter 10: Inspections, Appraisals, and Due Diligence

This is the part of the process where buyers often feel the most anxious — and usually for no good reason, once they understand what's actually happening and why. Let's take the mystery out of it.

## The Home Inspection

A general home inspection is your chance to have a qualified, neutral professional examine the home's condition before you're fully committed. In Southern Arizona, a good inspector will pay particular attention to:

- **Roofing** — sun exposure here is intense, and roofs age differently than in milder climates
- **HVAC systems** — air conditioning isn't a luxury here, it's essential infrastructure, and an aging or undersized system is a real cost to understand upfront
- **Foundation and drainage** — including how the property handles the region's occasional intense monsoon rains
- **Pool and spa equipment**, if applicable — a meaningful cost center if it needs repair or replacement

I recommend attending your inspection in person when possible. Seeing issues firsthand, and asking the inspector questions directly, gives you a far better feel for what's minor and what's significant than reading the report alone.

## Specialty Inspections

Depending on the property, you may also want:

- A **pool/spa inspection** from a specialist, beyond what a general inspector covers
- A **termite/pest inspection** — common and often required by lenders in this region
- A **well or septic inspection**, for more rural properties outside municipal water and sewer systems
- A **roof-specific inspection** for older homes or if the general inspector flags concerns

## What Happens When Something Turns Up

Almost every inspection finds *something* — that's normal, and it doesn't mean the deal is in trouble. Once we have the report, we have a few paths:

1. **Request repairs** from the seller before closing
2. **Request a credit** at closing to handle repairs yourself afterward
3. **Renegotiate price** to reflect the issue
4. **Walk away**, if the issue is significant enough and within your contingency period

I'll help you separate genuinely significant findings from normal wear-and-tear items that exist in every home, so you can respond proportionately rather than reactively.

## The Appraisal

If you're financing, your lender requires an independent appraisal to confirm the home's value supports the loan. In a rising or highly competitive market, it's possible for a home to appraise below the agreed purchase price. If that happens, your options include:

- Negotiating with the seller to reduce the price to match the appraisal
- Making up the difference in cash yourself
- Challenging the appraisal with additional comparable sales data
- Walking away, if your contract includes an appraisal contingency

This is a stressful moment when it happens, but it's also a manageable one with the right guidance — I've navigated this exact situation many times.

## Due Diligence Beyond the Physical Home

Especially for buyers relocating from out of state, due diligence should also include:

- Reviewing HOA documents and financials, if applicable, for upcoming assessments or reserve shortfalls
- Confirming property tax history and any pending reassessments
- Understanding utility providers and typical costs for the specific area

Taking this seriously now is what prevents unpleasant surprises after you own the home.
